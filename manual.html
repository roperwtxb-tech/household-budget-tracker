Start here

This app is one shared picture of our money, on both our phones at once.

Whatever one of us types shows up on the other's phone within a second or two. There's no "my copy" and "your copy" — it's one set of numbers we both edit. If Sean marks the electric bill paid at the feed store, Jessica sees it paid before she gets home.

It doesn't connect to any bank. Nothing is automatic. That's on purpose — we type the numbers in ourselves, which takes a few minutes a week and means we actually know what's in there.

Opening it

Tap the house icon on your home screen. If it asks for a password, that's the household password — same one for both of us. Tick Stay signed in on this device and it won't ask again on that phone.

The one indicator worth knowing

Under the screen title in the top-left there's a small dot. Green + "Live" means the app is connected and both phones are in sync. Red + "Offline" means it can't reach the internet — anything you type may not save. Get back on signal and it reconnects on its own.

First-time setup

One-time, about twenty minutes. Do it in this order — each step makes the next screen actually useful.

  1. Say whose phone this is The app asks the first time it opens. Tap Sean or Jessica. That's what labels who made each change, so we can see when the other has already handled something.
  2. Fix the accounts Accounts tab. Eight placeholders are waiting. Tap each one, give it the real name and today's balance. Delete any we don't have.
    For credit cards and loans, type what we owe as a positive number — the app knows those count against us. While you're in there, add the interest rate and monthly payment if you know them; that's what produces the payoff estimates.
  3. Set the budget Budget tab. Twenty categories are pre-made, all at $0. Tap the ones we actually use and put in a monthly amount. Hit Retire on the ones we don't — that hides them without deleting the history.
  4. Add the bills Bills tab → + New bill. Name, amount, when it's next due, how often it repeats, and which budget category it belongs to.
    This is the step that pays off fastest — the moment bills are in, the dashboard starts warning us before things are due.
  5. Set the sinking funds More → Sinking funds. Five are pre-made. Put a monthly contribution on each one. (See Sinking funds if that idea is new.)
  6. Enter the paychecks More → Income → + New income. Source, amount, date, and how often.
  7. Take the first snapshot More → Net worth → Snapshot now. That plants the first dot on the trend line. It'll look like nothing until there are two or three.

The weekly rhythm

Once it's set up, using it looks like this:

As it happens Spent money? Hit the orange + button, punch in the amount and category. Ten seconds. That's the whole habit.
Once a week Open Weekly review together and work down the six steps. Five minutes.
Once a month The dashboard prompts to add the sinking fund contributions — one tap. Glance at Spending trends to see where things drifted.
If you only do one thing

Do the weekly review. Everything else in the app is either feeding it or reading out of it. Skip the daily logging for a week and you'll survive; skip the review for a month and the numbers stop being true.

The orange + floating button is Quick Add and follows you everywhere. Top-right, ◐ flips between light and dark, and ☰ jumps to the More menu.

On Bills, Budget and Income there are ‹ › arrows at the top for moving between months. Wandered off? A Back to this month button appears.

Quick Add — the orange + button

Built for standing in a checkout line. Three tabs:

Expense

Amount, what it was for, category. Account and date are optional — date defaults to today. Tap Add and it's logged against that category's budget.

Income

Same thing for money coming in — a side job, a sale, birthday money.

Bill

A list of everything unpaid from the last 45 days and the next 30. Tap one and it's marked paid. That's it — no form.

Transfer

Move money between two of our own accounts in one entry. See Transfers.

Don't do both

If you mark a bill paid here, don't also log an expense for it. The app counts both, so the same money would hit the budget twice and the category would look overspent when it isn't.

Simple rule: bills get marked paid, everything else gets logged as an expense.

Bills

How repeating bills work

You enter a bill once with how often it repeats, and the app generates every future due date from that. The electric bill isn't twelve entries — it's one entry that shows up monthly, forever.

Marking it paid applies to that month only. Next month it comes back unpaid, as it should. Options are one-time, weekly, every 2 weeks, twice a month, monthly, quarterly, every 6 months, and yearly.

Paying one

Tap the green Pay button on that row. Marked the wrong one? Undo puts it back.

Editing a bill

Tap the bill's name (not the Pay button). Changing the amount here changes it for all future months.

Reading the tags

🔴 OverduePast its due date, still unpaid
🟡 in 3 daysDue within a week
⚪ UpcomingDue later this month
🟢 ✓ PaidDone for this cycle
autoMarked autopay — still needs marking paid so the budget sees it
About autopay

The autopay flag is just a reminder that we don't have to go pay it by hand. The app can't see our bank, so an autopay bill still has to be marked paid for the money to count against the budget. The weekly review is where that gets caught.

Budget

Each category has a monthly plan. The bar fills as the money goes out — green, then yellow past 85%, then red over.

Where "actual" comes from

Two places, added together:

  • Expenses logged with Quick Add against that category
  • Bills marked paid that belong to that category

Which is exactly why you don't want to do both for the same purchase.

Rollover

Switched on per category, with the ↻ symbol next to the name.

Off (the normal way): the category resets to its planned amount on the 1st. Anything unspent is gone. Right for groceries — under-spending one month doesn't earn us $200 of extra groceries in March.

On: whatever's left gets added to next month's plan. Budget $150 for home repairs and spend nothing? April has $300. Right for the categories where the whole point is building up for a big one.

It applies by itself the first time the app is opened in a new month. There's also a manual Roll leftovers in from… button at the bottom of the category list.

Currently rolling over: Home Repairs, Vehicle/Auto, Medical, Clothing, Gifts, Ranch/Livestock, Miscellaneous. Change any of them by tapping the category.

Categories

Add with + New category, edit by tapping. You can't delete one — you Retire it, which hides it while keeping past months accurate. Retired ones sit at the bottom with a Restore button.

Transactions

Below the categories, everything logged this month. Tap any one to fix or delete it.

Accounts & debt

Two tiles at the top — what we own, what we owe. Then accounts, then debts.

The one rule for balances

For a credit card or loan, enter what we owe as a plain positive number. $4,800 on a card is 4800, not -4800. The app already knows debts count against us; a minus sign makes it think the card owes us money.

Adding a card or loan

The Accounts card at the top holds checking, savings, cash and investments. Cards and loans live in the Debt payoff card below it, and each has its own + link:

  • + New account — checking, savings, cash, investment, property
  • + New card or loan — credit cards, auto loans, mortgages

Either one opens the same form; the second just starts with the type already set to credit card and the payoff fields showing. You can switch the type in the form at any point — change an account to a card and it moves itself down into Debt payoff.

Debt payoff

Add an interest rate and a monthly payment to any debt and the app works out when it'll be gone and roughly how much interest we'll pay getting there. Bump the payment up and watch the date move — that's the useful part.

If it says "payment does not cover interest," that's not a bug. It means the balance is growing faster than the payment shrinks it, and that debt never gets paid off at that amount. Worth knowing plainly.

Account types

Checking, savings, cash, investment/retirement, and property/vehicle count as things we own. Credit card and loan count as things we owe. Property is for a house or a truck we own outright — pair it with the loan against it and net worth comes out right.

Two accounts with the same name

If two accounts share a name — a checking and a savings at the same credit union, say — the app adds the type so you can tell them apart: TTFCU (Checking) and TTFCU (Savings). It only does this when there's an actual clash.

Account history

Tap any account to see everything that's happened to it.

This is the fix for the two-of-us problem: you go to update the checking balance, and the history says "Jessica updated 20 minutes ago." So you don't do it again and end up with a number that's been adjusted twice.

Each account's page shows the current balance, three buttons, and the full history underneath. The account list itself also shows who touched each one last, so you often don't even need to open it.

What's in the history

✎ Balance updatedSomeone typed a new balance — with the old and new figures
⇄ TransferMoney moved to or from another account
🧾 TransactionAn expense or income logged with Quick Add
📅 Bill paidA bill marked paid from this account
💵 IncomeMoney marked received into this account

Every line shows the date and which of us did it.

The "doesn't match" box

Sometimes an orange box appears at the top saying logged activity doesn't match the balance. It's not an error. It's the difference between two honest numbers:

  • The balance — what you last typed in from the bank
  • What we've logged since — the groceries, the bills marked paid, the paycheck

So if checking says $750 and we've logged $96 of groceries since the last update, the box points out it should be more like $654. Two possible reasons: the card hasn't cleared the bank yet, or the balance is stale.

Why this is useful

It's the closest thing to a second opinion the app can give. When the gap is small or explainable, everything's fine. When it's big and surprising, something got logged twice or missed — and you've caught it early.

Tap Update balance once you've checked the bank. The gap resets to zero and starts tracking fresh from there.

Naming your phone

The first time the app opens on a phone it asks which of us it belongs to. That's what puts the names in the history. It's stored on that phone only — no extra login, the shared password is unchanged. Change it later under More → Settings → This phone.

Transfers

Moving money between our own accounts — one entry instead of two.

Before, moving $500 from checking to Robinhood meant subtracting $500 from one and adding $500 to the other, and hoping you didn't fat-finger one of them. Now it's one entry: from, to, amount.

Where to find it

  • Accounts tab → the green ⇄ Transfer between accounts button at the top
  • The orange + button → Transfer tab
  • Any account's own page → ⇄ Transfer

Before you confirm, it shows you both balances and what they'll become. Worth a two-second glance — it also warns you if a transfer would push an account below zero.

Paying a credit card or loan

Transfer to a credit card or loan and the app treats it as a payment: money leaves the account it came from, and the amount owed goes down. You don't type a minus sign anywhere.

Why a card payment isn't "spending"

Paying $500 toward a credit card doesn't count against any budget category, and that's deliberate. Whatever you bought on that card was already counted as spending when you bought it. Counting the payment too would count the same money twice.

Same reasoning for moving money into savings — you haven't spent it, you've just moved it. Your net worth doesn't budge on a transfer, which is the giveaway that no money actually left the household.

What it won't let you do

  • Transfer an account to itself
  • Transfer $0
  • Transfer without picking both accounts

Each of those gets a plain message instead of silently doing something strange.

Income

More → Income. Paychecks and one-offs, with the same repeat options as bills.

Each entry is either expected or received. Mark it received when it actually lands — step 3 of the weekly review walks through anything outstanding.

Savings goals

Something specific we're saving toward, with a finish line. A tractor, a trip, a new roof.

Give it a name, target amount, what's saved already, and optionally a date. With a date set, the app shows what we'd need to put away monthly to make it — "$717/mo to hit Jun 2027."

Put money in with + Add money on the goal.

Sinking funds

Money set aside monthly for something that isn't monthly.

Property taxes don't care that they're a big number. They show up once a year and wreck that month. Same with Christmas, or the vet bill that isn't routine but is inevitable. Budget month to month only, and these permanently feel like emergencies — even though not one of them is a surprise.

So flip it. Take the yearly cost, divide by twelve, set that aside every month. When the bill comes, the money's already there.

An example

Property taxes run $3,840 a year. Instead of one brutal month, put $320 aside monthly. When the bill lands, it's covered, and that month feels like any other.

Fund vs. goal — what's the difference?

Sinking fundSavings goal
ShapeA cycle that repeatsA finish line you cross once
ExampleChristmas, property taxes, vet billsNew tractor, $12,000
After spending itStarts refilling for next timeDone — goal complete

Rule of thumb: "this happens again" is a fund. "We're saving up for this" is a goal.

Using them

  • + Add — put money in by hand
  • − Spend — the expense hit; draw the fund down
  • Once a month the dashboard offers to add every fund's contribution at once
These are labels, not accounts

The money doesn't actually move anywhere. A sinking fund is a label on money already sitting in savings. If there's $9,200 in savings and $695 across the funds, that $695 is part of the $9,200 — already spoken for.

So don't add a fund contribution and also subtract it from the savings balance, or it's counted twice. The account balance is the truth; the funds just say how much of it is claimed.

Picking the number: yearly cost ÷ 12 where we know it. For the fuzzy ones like car repair, guess based on a bad year and adjust once we've watched it a while.

Net worth

Everything we own minus everything we owe. One number for whether we're moving forward.

It doesn't record itself — hit Snapshot now and it stores today's figure. Do it during the weekly review and the trend builds on its own. Two snapshots minimum before a line appears; a handful before it means anything.

A negative number early on is normal, especially with a mortgage. The direction matters more than the number.

Spending trends

More → Spending trends. Where the money actually went, month over month.

Stacked bars, one per month, colored by category — switch between 3, 6 and 12 months. Tap a bar for the exact breakdown. Table view shows the same thing as plain numbers if that reads easier.

Underneath, the biggest movers versus last month. Green means we spent less, red means more.

Reading the current month

The month we're in is always partial, so its bar looks short and every category looks "down." The app says which day of the month we're on underneath. Compare finished months to finished months.

This is the screen that answers "where is it all going?" rather than "did we hit the number?" Give it three or four months before drawing conclusions.

Weekly review

The five minutes that keeps the rest honest.

More → Weekly review, or the green button at the bottom of the dashboard. Six steps, top to bottom:

  1. Reconcile the balances Open the bank app and type in what each account actually says. This is the most important step in the whole app — everything else is built on these numbers being real. Hit Save balances.
    Each line shows who updated it last and when, so if one of you did it this morning you can skip that account.
  2. Clear the week's bills Anything overdue or due in the next 7 days. Tap Pay on what's been paid — including autopay ones.
  3. Confirm income Paychecks that landed get marked Received.
  4. Fund the sinking funds Once a month it'll offer the contributions. The rest of the weeks it just says they're already in.
  5. Check the budget Anything over gets listed. No lecture — just a heads up while there's still month left to adjust.
  6. Snapshot net worth Shows assets, debt and the total, then Save snapshot for today. One tap, and the trend line grows.

Pick a time and keep it. Sunday evening, coffee Saturday morning — doesn't matter which, matters that it's the same one.

Backup & settings

Backing up

More → Backup & export:

  • Export JSON — a complete copy of everything. This is the real backup; it can be restored.
  • Export CSV — for opening in Excel or Google Sheets.
  • Restore backup — loads a JSON file back in.

Worth doing every few months. Save it somewhere that isn't just the phone.

Settings

  • This phone — whose phone this is, used to label changes in account history. Stored on the phone, not shared.
  • Change household password — needs the current one first. Whoever changes it has to tell the other, or they're locked out.
  • Toggle light / dark — same as the ◐ button up top. Set per phone.
  • Check for app updates — the app updates itself when reopened, so this is only for impatience. It tells you either the new version it's installing or that you're already current. The version running is printed underneath.
  • Sign out on this device — only that phone. Everything stays put.

Rules that matter

  1. Mark bills paid, log everything else as an expense. Never both for the same purchase — it double-counts.
  2. Debts go in as positive numbers. $4,800 owed is 4800.
  3. Sinking funds are labels, not accounts. The money is already in savings; don't subtract it twice.
  4. Use Transfer for money moving between our accounts. Don't log an expense on one side and income on the other — that's not spending, and it would wreck the budget.
  5. Check the account history before updating a balance. If it says the other one of us just did it, it's done.
  6. Reconcile weekly. Typed balances drift from real ones faster than you'd think.
  7. Green dot before you type. Red means offline and it may not save.
  8. Retire, don't delete. Retiring a category keeps last year's numbers true.

Nothing here can really be broken

Every number can be edited or deleted, and there's a full backup a couple of taps away. Type the wrong thing, tap it, fix it. Nobody's going to break this.
